Real conditions are rarely a single yes-or-no. “Allow access if the user is logged in and verified” combines two propositions into one. Logical connectives are the glue words that do this, and each one has a precise meaning defined by a truth table.

NOT (¬)

Negation flips a truth value. If P is true then ¬P is false, and the other way around. In most languages this is !P.

AND (∧)

P ∧ Q is true only when both P and Q are true. In code: P && Q.

OR (∨)

P ∨ Q is true when at least one of P and Q is true, including when both are. In code: P || Q. This is the inclusive “or”. The exclusive “or” (XOR), true when exactly one is true, is a different connective.

The truth tables

P  Q | P ∧ Q | P ∨ Q      P | ¬P
T  T |   T   |   T        T |  F
T  F |   F   |   T        F |  T
F  T |   F   |   T
F  F |   F   |   F

Order of evaluation

Just like multiplication before addition, NOT binds tightest, then AND, then OR. So P ∨ Q ∧ R means P ∨ (Q ∧ R). When a condition is important, add parentheses anyway. Your teammates and your future self will thank you.

Key idea: AND is strict (everything must hold), OR is generous (one is enough), NOT flips.

Try predicting the value of (P ∧ Q) ∨ ¬R when P = T, Q = F, R = F. Since Q is false the AND branch is false, but ¬R is true, so the OR is true. You will explore this exact expression in the next lesson.
