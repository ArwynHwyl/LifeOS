“Drive 50 kilometres” is an incomplete instruction. Which way? “Drive 50 kilometres north” is complete. Some quantities need a direction to make sense, and vectors are the mathematical tool for them.

Scalars and vectors

  • A scalar has only a size (magnitude): temperature, mass, time, speed.
  • A vector has both magnitude and direction: velocity, force, displacement, acceleration.

Speed is a scalar (60 km/h). Velocity is a vector (60 km/h heading east). Two cars can have the same speed and very different velocities.

Drawing and naming vectors

We draw a vector as an arrow. The length shows the magnitude, and the arrowhead shows the direction. The end without the arrowhead is the tail (starting point) and the pointed end is the head (end point). In print we write vectors in bold, such as a, or with an arrow above the letter. The magnitude is written |a|.

Equal vectors

Two vectors are equal when they have the same magnitude and the same direction. Where they are drawn does not matter: sliding an arrow across the page without turning it gives the same vector.

Two basic operations

  • Adding vectors: place the tail of the second arrow at the head of the first. The sum runs from the first tail to the second head. This is the “tip-to-tail” rule.
  • Scalar multiplication: multiplying by a number k stretches the vector by |k|. If k is negative, the direction reverses.
Key idea: a vector is a quantity that needs two things to be described: how much, and which way.

Example: walking 3 km east then 4 km north gives a total displacement of 5 km in a north-eastern direction, even though you walked 7 km. You will see exactly why in the next lesson.
