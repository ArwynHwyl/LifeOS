Every square matrix has a single number attached to it that reveals a lot: the determinant. It tells you whether the matrix can be undone, how much it stretches space, and whether a system of equations has a unique solution.

The 2 × 2 determinant

For a 2 × 2 matrix, multiply along the main diagonal and subtract the product along the other diagonal:

| a  b |
| c  d |   =  ad − bc

Example

| 3  1 |
| 2  4 |   =  3×4 − 1×2 = 12 − 2 = 10

What the number means

  • The absolute value of the determinant is the area scale factor: a unit square is mapped to a parallelogram of that area.
  • A negative determinant means the shape is also flipped (mirrored).
  • A determinant of zero means the matrix squashes the plane onto a line. It has no inverse and is called singular.

Larger matrices

For a 3 × 3 matrix, expand along a row: multiply each entry by the determinant of the smaller matrix left after deleting its row and column, and alternate the signs + − +. The idea is the same: a single number that summarises the matrix.

Key idea: a matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant is not zero.

Try it

Adjust a, b, c, and d until ad − bc equals 5. Many combinations work. For instance a = 1 and d = 5 with b = c = 0 gives 5, and so does a = 3, d = 2, b = 1, c = 1, because 6 − 1 = 5.
