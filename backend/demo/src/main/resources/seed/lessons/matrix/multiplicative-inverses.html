Matrices cannot be divided, but many of them can be “undone”. The undoing matrix is called the inverse, and it is the key to solving equations that involve matrices.

Definition

The inverse of a square matrix A is a matrix A⁻¹ such that AA⁻¹ = I and A⁻¹A = I, where I is the identity matrix. It plays the role of the reciprocal: just as 5 × (1/5) = 1, we have A A⁻¹ = I.

When does it exist?

A square matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant is nonzero. Matrices with determinant 0 are singular and have no inverse.

The 2 × 2 formula

     [ a  b ]                 1     [  d  −b ]
A =  [ c  d ]      A⁻¹ = ───────  [ −c   a ]
                          ad − bc

Swap the diagonal entries, change the sign of the other two, and divide everything by the determinant.

Worked example

Let A = [[3, 1], [2, 4]]. Its determinant is 3×4 − 1×2 = 10, so:

A⁻¹ = (1/10) [  4  −1 ]  =  [  0.4  −0.1 ]
             [ −2   3 ]     [ −0.2   0.3 ]

Check the first entry of AA⁻¹: 3×0.4 + 1×(−0.2) = 1.2 − 0.2 = 1. Good.

Key idea: the inverse undoes the matrix, and it exists only when the determinant is not zero.

Also remember (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹. The order reverses, just like taking off your shoes and socks after putting them on.
