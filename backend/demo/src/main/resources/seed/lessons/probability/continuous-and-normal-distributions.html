Height, weight, reaction time, measurement error: these can take any value in a range, not just whole numbers. Such variables are continuous, and they need a different way of describing probability.

Probability as area

For a continuous variable the chance of hitting one exact value is essentially zero, so instead we ask for the chance of landing in an interval. It equals the area under the curve above that interval. The total area under the curve is always 1.

The normal distribution

The most important continuous distribution is the normal distribution, the bell curve. It is symmetric about its mean μ, and its width is set by the standard deviation σ. Countless natural quantities are approximately normal.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

  • About 68% of values lie within 1σ of the mean.
  • About 95% lie within 2σ.
  • About 99.7% lie within 3σ.

Z-scores

To compare values on different scales, convert to a z-score, the number of standard deviations from the mean:

z = (x − μ) / σ

If exam marks have μ = 70 and σ = 10, then a mark of 90 has z = 2. It is two standard deviations above average, better than about 97.5% of students.

Key idea: for continuous data, probability is area under the curve, and the normal curve is the model to know.
