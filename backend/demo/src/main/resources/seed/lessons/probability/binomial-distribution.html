Many real situations repeat the same yes-or-no trial again and again: a basketball player’s free throws, a machine producing defective or good parts, customers who click or do not click. The binomial distribution counts the number of successes in such a series.

When does it apply?

All four conditions must hold:

  1. There is a fixed number of trials, n.
  2. Each trial has only two outcomes: success or failure.
  3. The trials are independent of each other.
  4. The probability of success p is the same on every trial.

The formula

If X is the number of successes, then

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

where C(n, k) = n! / (k!(n − k)!) counts the ways to place k successes among n trials.

Worked example

Flip a fair coin 5 times. What is the probability of exactly 3 heads?

C(5, 3) = 10
P(X = 3) = 10 × (0.5)³ × (0.5)² = 10/32 = 0.3125

Now a player with a 70% free-throw rate takes 4 shots. The probability of making exactly 3 is C(4,3)(0.7)³(0.3) = 4 × 0.343 × 0.3 ≈ 0.412.

Mean and spread

mean μ = n · p
variance = n · p · (1 − p)

For 5 fair coin flips, the expected number of heads is 5 × 0.5 = 2.5.

Key idea: count the ways to arrange the successes, then multiply by the probability of one such arrangement.
